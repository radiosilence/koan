kōan

It's a music player and server, for local collections and remote ones (anything OpenSubsonic). Remote libraries sit behind a fairly aggressive local cache. It handles multi-terabyte libraries with ease and has all the core audio features you'd want, like gapless and bit-perfect output (where the system allows). It's built from 25 years of messing about with music, being annoyed with pretty much everything, and wanting my dream application.

The idea is to be fully compatible with the existing ecosystem while bringing the decent UX and modern ideas that professionally made streaming services have. It started as a little cross-platform CLI and TUI app on a Rust core. Now there's a native SwiftUI macOS app (no Electron) that links that core directly, an iOS app, a server, and an Apple TV app on TestFlight. The UX takes what I like about Apple Music and fb2k and fixes the things I thought were dumb. The point is to do the basics properly before adding features, and I'm really proud of it.

I wanted UX that makes it easy as hell to do what you want, while staying SUPER low on resources (and now battery). And I wanted the stuff you don't really see in the self-hosted space: device control and handoff, EQ profiles and convolution, DLNA output (with EQ!), and a cache built for a commuter who often has no signal, so you never have to remember to download your whole queue first. The best of every world, why not?

My philosophy is that you should lead with your opinions, but let people customise and tweak them to match theirs.

Because it grew organically, and I've insisted on staying compatible with OpenSubsonic, every part works on its own. You can use the macOS app with Navidrome. You can use the server with Arpeggi. Or you can use kōan all the way down and get the non-standard (sorry) features such as remote control. I think a shared standard like OpenSubsonic is incredibly important so everyone plays nicely together, but it shouldn't stop us experimenting to compete with how well the professional streaming platforms integrate.

One thing I've noticed: when you mix self-hosted apps with proprietary tech, say AirPlay from the fantastically solid play:Sub, you're treated as a second-class citizen. The audio has to stream over the wire from your phone, which is laggy. With kōan's remote features, the device you send it to plays its own copy.

I did use AI-assisted coding for this project. I've been building fairly high-quality software for a loooong time (decades) before AI existed, and I'd like to think the decisions reflect that rather than vibing slop. I probably could have written it myself, but I wanted to step back and be more of an architect, technical lead and product owner than the person typing out the code, as I'm just one person.

If it gets traction I'll happily look at more platforms like Android, but I'm already out $99 for an Apple Developer account, so I'm not shelling out for an Android phone just yet.

What is unusual about it

  • Your DAC runs at the track's rate. Every track flips the device to its own sample rate. Nothing gets resampled to suit the device, and if a device refuses, the format badge tells you so. Playback
  • Gapless survives you messing with the queue. The decoder lines the next track up early, so if you then reorder or remove it, playback restarts at the playhead with your edit, instead of playing the thing you just got rid of. Playback
  • Room and headphone correction, per output. Roon filter packs, REW and rePhase impulses, CamillaDSP and Equalizer APO configs, AutoEQ: they all import as they are. A device without a profile runs nothing at all. Equalisation and convolution
  • Network amps get the actual file. A UPnP/DLNA renderer gets handed the file itself. Or, if you've given it a correction profile, one FLAC stream for the whole queue, gapless whether the amp can do gapless or not. Amplifiers and streamers
  • Music moves between devices, properly. It picks up exactly where it stopped, straight away if the other device already has the track, and with none of AirPlay's buffering. That device plays its own copy, so it keeps going if your phone sleeps or drops signal. Devices on the same network can control each other whoever's signed in, and you can share a device with another account on your server to control it from anywhere. One device at a time though, no multiroom yet. Controlling and moving
  • Built for losing signal on the Tube. While you've got signal, the queue gets fetched ahead of the playhead (current track first) as far as the cache limit allows, so it keeps playing underground, and anything you've played once plays again offline. A long queue can't push the cache past its limit either. Cache management
  • Sync is fast. A full sync is about a second per 10,000 tracks on an iPhone against a kōan server: songs come a page at a time, so it's about a hundred requests for fifty thousand tracks rather than one per album. And if the server's library hasn't changed, it doesn't walk it at all. Remote servers
  • A whole ecosystem that's still plain OpenSubsonic. Apps, terminal UI, web UI, share links and server all come from kōan, but the apps play from Navidrome too, and Play:Sub or any other Subsonic app plays from a kōan server. As a server
  • Light on the machine. Nothing polls. The player sleeps until something actually happens, and the analyser stops when nothing's drawing it. Playing, with a 51,000-track library loaded, the Mac app sits at about 200 MB and 1% of one core, and the server holding that library idles at 30 MB and a thousandth of a core. The app is 33 MB installed and the command line is a single 45 MB binary. The pieces
  • An assistant as a remote. Add the server to Claude as a connector and sign in with your kōan account, no gateway or identity provider faff. It plays on whichever phone or Mac you name, and builds playlists that reach every device and even hold places for albums still downloading, filling them in when they land. It can never move, rename or delete a file, whatever the account. With an assistant
  • Inviting someone is one link. Open it on a phone or Mac with kōan and it signs that device in with a key of its own and syncs the library, nothing to type. Each device's key can be revoked on its own, and changing the password kills every link already sent. Invites
  • One Rust core, linked right in. The SwiftUI apps on macOS and iOS call the audio engine in-process. No Electron, and no daemon or port sitting between the interface and the sound. The pieces

If you want to see how all that stacks up against NaviBeat, Navidrome, Jellyfin, Roon and the rest, How kōan compares goes feature by feature, including the stuff kōan doesn't do (yet).

The basics, done properly

  • Compilations stay one album. Albums group by album artist, so a various-artists record doesn't explode into one album per track artist, and two editions with different MusicBrainz releases stay two.
  • The queue comes back how you left it. Restart and it's all there, at the point in the track where you stopped, and it only plays if it was playing.
  • You can queue a track twice. Every entry has its own id, so moving or removing one copy leaves the other alone, and you can undo the last 100 queue edits.
  • One artist is one artist. SIGUR RÓS on a server and Sigur Rós on disk are the same act, whatever the case or Unicode form.
  • Your tags are left alone. kōan reads them and never writes them back. Favourites belong to the track, so they follow it when the file moves.

The pieces

Every part of kōan is the same Rust core with a different front on it. The apps link the core in-process, so nothing between the interface and the audio engine is a network hop.

  • macOS app. SwiftUI, for the machine you actually sit and listen at. Plays local folders, a server, or both merged into one library.
  • iOS app. The same SwiftUI app in a phone's shell. A phone doesn't have a music folder, so it plays from a server and keeps what it's played in a local cache.
  • Terminal UI and CLI. koan on macOS and Linux: the player in a terminal, plus scanning, search, file organisation and the server itself.
  • Server. koan --headless, or the container image, on whatever machine holds the music. It serves a web UI, share links, an OpenSubsonic API and MCP.

If it's one computer with the music on it, the app on its own is all you need. If you want to listen from a phone, another computer or a browser, run the server where the music lives and point everything else at it. The apps sync its library, stream what they play and cache it, so a record you've played once plays again with no signal. Any Subsonic client works against it too.

Install

macOS app

Download Koan.dmg, open it and drag kōan into Applications. It's signed and notarised. Or with Homebrew:

brew install --cask radiosilence/koan/koan-app

You might have to update Homebrew's trust settings to trust the tap.

Requires macOS 26 or later.

iOS app

Build it from source with Xcode and put it on a phone plugged into your Mac, signed with your own (free) Apple developer team:

git clone https://github.com/radiosilence/koan.git && cd koan
just ios-phone

Needs iOS 26 or later, and a kōan, Navidrome or other Subsonic server to play from.

Command line and terminal UI

# mise (recommended)
mise use -g github:radiosilence/koan@latest

# homebrew
brew install radiosilence/koan/koan

# or via cargo
cargo install koan-cli

# or build from source
git clone https://github.com/radiosilence/koan.git && cd koan
cargo install --path crates/koan-cli

It's a single binary. macOS needs nothing else; Linux needs the ALSA and D-Bus development headers:

# Debian/Ubuntu
sudo apt install libasound2-dev libdbus-1-dev

# Fedora
sudo dnf install alsa-lib-devel dbus-devel

# Arch
sudo pacman -S alsa-lib dbus

Setting it up

One computer

In the macOS app it's all in Settings: Library points kōan at your music and scans it, and playback and devices have their own panes. No terminal needed.

Or from the command line:

koan config init   # create the config dir and a commented template
# edit ~/.config/koan/config.local.toml:
#   [library]
#   folders = ["/path/to/your/music"]
koan scan          # index your library
koan               # launch the TUI

space pauses, < and > skip, p picks tracks, a picks albums, q quits.

A server for everything else

Run the container on the machine with the music. It doesn't need a sound card, keeps its config, index and keys in /config, and only ever reads the library.

docker run -d --name koan -p 4000:4000 \
  -v koan-config:/config \
  -v /path/to/music:/music:ro \
  -e 'KOAN_LIBRARY__FOLDERS=["/music"]' \
  -e 'KOAN_GRAPHQL__ALLOWED_HOSTS=["music.example.com"]' \
  -e KOAN_SHARING__PUBLIC_URL=https://music.example.com \
  -e KOAN_SUBSONIC__ENABLED=true \
  ghcr.io/radiosilence/koan:latest

docker exec -it koan koan auth setup   # the admin account

Stick it behind a reverse proxy with TLS at the hostname you listed, or use the Docker Compose file, which runs Caddy in front and sorts the certificate out itself. The server refuses any Host it wasn't given, and share links are built on public_url. The first admin can only be made from a shell on the server, like above; after that, add people from the web UI's Users page or with koan auth create-user. Without a container, koan --headless --bind 0.0.0.0 does the same with the config in ~/.config/koan. Running a server has the details, and Migrating from Navidrome translates a Navidrome setup.

Then sign in from each client with a kōan account:

  • Browser: open the hostname.
  • macOS and iOS apps: Settings → Server.
  • Terminal: koan remote login https://music.example.com <user>, then koan remote sync.
  • Other Subsonic apps: the same URL and account.

A Navidrome or other Subsonic server works in the kōan server's place for the apps and terminal UI; the web UI, share pages, linked apps and MCP are kōan's own. Local and remote tracks merge into one library, local files win for playback, and remote tracks stream while they download.

On Kubernetes

Each release publishes a Pulumi component at the same version, @radiosilence/koan-pulumi, on GitHub Packages. It builds the Deployment, its Service and a NetworkPolicy that only lets your ingress in:

# .npmrc (GitHub Packages wants a token even for public packages)
@radiosilence:registry=https://npm.pkg.github.com
import { createKoan } from "@radiosilence/koan-pulumi";

createKoan(provider, "media", {
  hostname: "music.example.com",
  library: { hostPath: "/mnt/music" },
  state: { hostPath: "/var/lib/koan" },
  nodeSelector: { "kubernetes.io/hostname": "media-node" },
});

Route the hostname to the koan Service with whatever ingress you run. Pin the package version and the server follows it, since the image tag is the package's version.

Filing new music

kōan reads tags as they are and won't rewrite them. For getting new music in, there's sift, a separate tool I wrote: it matches a download against MusicBrainz, tags it, embeds the cover and files it into the library, reading an existing beets config. A server watching that folder picks the album up on its own.

mise use -g github:radiosilence/sift
sift import ~/Downloads/some-album

What it does

Playback

  • Bit-perfect output through CoreAudio on macOS and ALSA on Linux. The device gets switched to the source's sample rate instead of resampling to reach it, and if a device refuses, the format badge says the output's resampled.
  • Gapless: the decoder runs ahead across track boundaries.
  • Shuffle and repeat, of the whole queue or one track. Repeats are gapless and each pass counts in history. Shuffle reorders the queue you can see, and turning it off puts it back in its original order.
  • Network amplifiers and streamers: the Mac app and the terminal UI play to UPnP/DLNA renderers, like a WiiM or an Arcam amp. The queue stays in kōan and the renderer gets sent the original file, so it's bit-perfect up to its DAC, and gapless where the renderer can do it. Give an amp its own EQ or convolution profile and kōan processes the queue itself and sends one continuous FLAC stream instead, gapless whatever the amp supports. See Playing on another device.
  • EQ and convolution, picked per output device, so headphones only get their correction when they're the output. Profiles import from what other tools write: Roon and Convolver configs, impulse responses from REW or rePhase, CamillaDSP and Equalizer APO (mixers, delays and graphic EQ included), and AutoEQ. A device without a profile runs nothing and stays bit-perfect, and the format badge tells you when audio's being processed. See Equalisation and convolution.
  • Sleep timer: after 15 to 60 minutes, fading out, or at the end of the track or record. It pauses and keeps the queue, and a phone controlling a Mac sets the Mac's. See Sleep timer.
  • Formats: FLAC, MP3, AAC, Vorbis, Opus, ALAC, ADPCM, WAV, AIFF and CAF, in Ogg, Matroska/WebM and MP4 containers.
  • ReplayGain in track and album modes, with peak limiting and a configurable pre-amp.
  • Media keys and now-playing through Control Center on macOS and MPRIS on Linux.

Library

  • Local and Subsonic/Navidrome in one library, synced when the server changes, with streaming, and favourites and playlists synced both ways.
  • A download cache with a size limit that copes with however long the queue is: tracks get fetched ahead of the cursor as far as the limit allows, played ones get evicted first, and tracks you downloaded to keep go last. See Cache management.
  • Search across the whole library, with SQLite full-text search.
  • Queue grouped by album, with drag reordering, multi-select, 100 levels of undo and Finder drag and drop. It survives restarts.
  • Playlists, ordered and named, exportable as M3U8. Smart playlists hold whatever matches a set of rules, and Navidrome's .nsp files and .m3u files in the library folders get imported. See Smart playlists.
  • Play history and Recently played, shared between an account's devices on a kōan server. Forget a play and it's forgotten on all of them.
  • Synced lyrics from LRCLIB, current line highlighted.
  • File organisation with sift's foobar2000-compatible format strings (%field%, [conditionals] and 59 $functions()). Every move gets previewed, collisions included, before anything's touched.

Automation

  • GraphQL API for full control alongside the app or TUI, or headless as a music server.
  • Subsonic-compatible API, so other Subsonic clients can play from a kōan server.
  • MCP for assistants: at /mcp on a server, signing in with a kōan account, or koan mcp for a desktop client. See below.
  • Accounts with Ed25519 JWTs, three roles and 1Password CLI integration.

Not there yet: tag editing. It's planned.

The macOS app

A SwiftUI app built for macOS 26. Browse and search the library (⌘K), build and reorder the queue, keep playlists, favourite tracks, albums and artists, read synced lyrics, dig through play history and what you played recently, set a sleep timer and reorganise files on disk. The Output menu sends the music to this Mac's audio devices or to UPnP amps on the network, each with its own EQ or convolution preset, and marks the speaker button whenever what you're hearing is processed. Once you've got another kōan device, Control drives that one instead, and Output then lists its outputs. First-run setup covers library folders and remote sign-in.

It links the Rust core in-process through FFI instead of talking to a server, so there's no daemon, port or auth surface between the interface and the audio engine, and CoreAudio output never leaves Rust. It shares one library and one config with the terminal UI, so a queue saved in one shows up in the other.

Two things it leaves to the terminal: visualisers, and running the server (koan --headless).

The iOS app

The macOS app's pages and engine in a phone's shell: a tab bar for the queue, the library, search and settings, and Now Playing with synced lyrics and AirPlay over the playing record's colour. It plays from a server and caches what it plays, so the rest of an album or queue carries on underground. On an iPad with room for a sidebar it goes for the Mac's layout. Output goes through the system mixer, so bit-perfect is a Mac and Linux thing only, sorry. EQ and convolution profiles follow the route, so your AirPods and your wired headphones each get their own.

The Apple TV app

The same pages and engine again, built around the remote: Now Playing first, the tabs across the top, a long press for a row's menu. It's mostly something your phone or Mac plays to, and it plays gaplessly through HDMI. Signing in doesn't involve typing a password with a remote: open kōan on a phone that's signed in, the TV finds your server, and you scan its code. No kōan on the phone? The code opens your server's own page instead. How signing in works.

The terminal UI

Where it all started: a full-screen Ratatui interface on macOS and Linux, with a transport bar with album art, an album-grouped queue, a fuzzy picker, a library browser, a lyrics panel, mouse support and 22 visualiser modes (because why not).

As a server

Run kōan on the machine that holds the music (koan --headless, or a container) and it serves the library to everything else. It stands in for Navidrome rather than needing it, and Migrating from Navidrome covers the move.

  • Web UI. Every album, artist and track on one page, search and a queue, laid out for a phone as well as a desktop. Filters apply as you type and live in the URL, so you can bookmark or send a filtered view. Favourites, Recently played and History are there like in the apps, with hearts and a track menu on every row. And playback is gapless, in the browser.
  • Share links. Share a track and you share its album, cued to that track. An album shares the album, and an artist their albums. The page plays without an account, and a pasted link unfurls with its cover.
  • OpenSubsonic API. Subsonic apps like Play:Sub, and kōan's own app and terminal UI on other machines, connect with a kōan account: by password, with an API key, or with an app password for apps that only sign in with a token. Ratings and bookmarks are kept per account, and a stream gets transcoded to Opus, MP3 or AAC for an app that asks for a lower bitrate.
  • Scrobbling. Each account can connect ListenBrainz, and the server sends every play its apps report, so you don't have to set it up in each app. See Scrobbling.
  • Accounts. Admin, user and read-only roles, plus API keys and app passwords you can revoke from the web UI's Account page.
  • Linked apps. kōan's Mac and iPhone apps keep a connection open to a kōan server, which makes each one a remote the server can drive: see what it's playing and its queue, start an album on it, queue, pause and skip. Playlist edits and library changes reach every device without a sync, and one that's away gets them when it next connects. A plain Subsonic server can only answer what an app asks it.
  • MCP. At /mcp, with the server as its own sign-in, so an assistant can browse the library and drive playback on any of your linked devices. See With an assistant.

With an assistant

A kōan server is an MCP server. Claude, or any assistant that speaks MCP, works with your library and with the kōan apps you listen on, as you. It can see every phone and computer linked to your account, what each is playing and what's queued, and send commands to whichever one you name.

What it's good for

  • Playing on your devices. "Play something like Polar Bear on my phone." It finds the music in your library and starts it on the iPhone; "pause the desktop", "skip this", "play the Phace remix next" go to whichever device you mean. If iOS has put the phone to sleep it gets a notification to tap, and queue changes wait for it to wake up.
  • Making playlists that reach every device. "Make me a playlist for a long drive, mostly instrumental, nothing over seven minutes." It researches what fits, picks it from your library in order, and the playlist shows up on your Mac and phone straight away, no sync needed.
  • Playlists that fill as the music arrives. For picks your library doesn't have yet, it can leave a placeholder in the playlist: when the album lands in your library, the tracks it wanted get added. You hear what's there now, and the rest turns up when it arrives.
  • Knowing what's on. "What's this song?" and "what's left in the queue on my phone?" read the device's own state.
  • Sharing. "Make a link to this album I can send a friend" creates a share page that plays without an account, once you've said yes to it.

Connecting

In Claude, open Settings → Connectors → Add custom connector and put in your server's address with /mcp on the end:

https://music.example.com/mcp

Claude opens a page on your server. Sign in with your kōan account, check it says claude.ai, and allow it. Other assistants that support OAuth connect the same way, and the server's web UI has an Assistants page with the address to copy. There's no gateway or identity provider involved: the server is its own sign-in, it just needs public_url set to the address it's reached at.

What it can and can't do

A connection acts as the account that approved it, on that account's devices and playlists. It can never move, rename or delete a file, or change where the library is. Those are refused through MCP whatever the account, because track titles and tags reach the model, and any of them could be carrying an instruction. An admin account connects with ordinary rights for the same reason, and its tokens work at /mcp and nowhere else.

No server? koan mcp runs the player on this machine for a desktop client like Claude Desktop. The MCP guide covers both.
